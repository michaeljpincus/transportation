Question 4

  1. Trip generation asks how many trips are produced in and attracted to each zone? It uses household and zonal characteristics (size, income, vehicles, workers, employment) to estimate trip productions and attractions. The output is the number of trips produced by and attracted to each traffic analysis zone (TAZ), usually by trip purpose.
  2. Trip distribution asks where do those trips go? It links productions to attractions, typically with a gravity model in which trips fall with travel time or cost and rise with the size of the destination. The output is an origin-destination (zone-to-zone) trip table.
  3. Mode choice asks how do travelers make each trip? It splits the O-D trips among modes (drive alone, carpool, transit, walk, bike) based on the cost, time and convenience of each mode and on traveler characteristics, often with a logit model. The output is O-D trip tables by mode (mode shares).
  4. Trip assignment asks which routes do trips take? It loads mode-specific trips onto the highway and transit networks, usually so that travel times are consistent with congestion (equilibrium). The output is volumes (flows) on each link or transit line, plus resulting travel times, speeds and congestion.

Question 5

  • Trip generation: The survey gives the number of trips each person and household made, along with household size, income, vehicles and workers. Cross-classifying or regressing trips per household on these characteristics (as in Questions 1-3) yields trip production rates. The survey can also validate a model by comparing predicted and observed trips per household.
  • Trip distribution: The survey records trip origins and destinations, so trips can be aggregated into an observed zone-to-zone trip table. That table can be used to estimate the friction (distance-decay) parameter of the gravity model, or to compare observed and predicted trip-length frequency distributions.
  • Mode choice: The survey records the mode used for every trip, together with traveler and household characteristics and each trip’s origin, destination and time. Observed mode shares can be used to estimate a mode choice model (for example, a logit model) or to check that predicted shares match observed shares.
  • Why the survey is less useful for trip assignment: Assignment predicts volumes on specific links and routes. A household survey samples only a small fraction of travelers and records trip endpoints and mode, usually not the path taken, so it cannot directly observe link flows or route choice. Those require traffic counts, transit boardings, GPS or probe data, and the network is then assigned and calibrated against them.

The estimated regression equation is

\[\widehat{\text{rider}} = 2159.3 + 0.2198(\text{pop}) + 0.7403(\text{jobs}) + 5767(\text{terminal}) + 680.3(\text{airport}).\]

Substituting 5,000 residents, 10,000 jobs, and 0 for both indicators:

\[\widehat{\text{rider}} = 2159.3 + 0.2198(5000) + 0.7403(10000) + 5767(0) + 680.3(0) \approx 10662.\]

The lower bound is negative, which is impossible for ridership, a sign that a linear model in levels is not well suited to these data.

At low predicted values the residuals are tightly packed near zero, while at higher predicted values they are much more spread out. If the model performed equally well across the full range of predicted ridership, the residuals would be scattered randomly around zero with roughly constant spread at every predicted value (homoscedastic), with no trend or curve.

Coefficients. In the linear model the coefficients are additive effects in riders. In the log-log model the coefficients on log(pop_halfmile) and log(jobs_halfmile) are elasticities: a 1% increase in nearby population is associated with a 0.09% change in ridership, and a 1% increase in nearby jobs with a 0.48% change, holding the other variables constant. The indicators are not logged, so they are multiplicative effects: terminal stations have about 59% higher ridership than non-terminal stations (p < 0.001, statistically significant at the 5% level), and airport stations about 63% higher (p = 0.099, not statistically significant at the 5% level).

Fit. The log-log model has an \(R^2\) of 0.316 compared with 0.386 for the linear model. These \(R^2\) values are measured on different dependent variables (ln ridership versus ridership), so they are only a rough comparison, and the residual standard errors cannot be compared directly at all.

Residuals. The linear model’s residuals fan out with predicted ridership (heteroscedasticity). The log-log residuals are evenly scattered around zero with roughly constant spread.

Conclusion. The log-log specification is more appropriate. Its residuals meet the constant-variance assumption, so its standard errors and \(p\)-values can be trusted. It cannot predict negative ridership. It also matches the distributions we saw earlier (skewed ridership, roughly normal ln ridership, and a roughly linear relationship in logs), and its elasticity coefficients are easy to interpret. One limitation is that stations with zero ridership, population or jobs are dropped from this model, because their logs are undefined.

Interpretation. The coefficient on parking_d is -0.0045. Because the dependent variable is ln(ridership) and the indicator is not logged, stations with park-and-ride facilities have about 0.5% lower ridership than stations without, holding population, jobs, terminal and airport status constant. That is effectively zero.

Significance and reasonableness. The coefficient is not statistically significant (\(p = 0.95\)), and its 95% confidence interval runs from about -0.14 to +0.13 (about -13% to +14% in ridership). We therefore find no evidence that park-and-ride facilities are associated with ridership once population, jobs, terminal and airport status are controlled for. A positive effect would have been plausible, since parking lets a station draw riders from beyond the half-mile catchment. The estimate may be near zero because the population and jobs measures already capture much of what differs between stations, or because park-and-ride stations tend to be in lower-density areas that offset any boost from parking. The sign is not meaningful given how close to zero the estimate is.

Effect on other coefficients. Adding the indicator barely changes the other estimates. The largest change in any coefficient is 0.4% (the intercept moves from 4.029 to 4.036, and the population, jobs, terminal and airport coefficients change by 0.2% or less), and no signs flip.

Model fit. Fit does not improve. Adjusted \(R^2\) falls slightly from 0.3124 to 0.3115, the F-test comparing the nested models gives \(p = 0.95\), and AIC increases from 1921.8 to 1923.8 (higher is worse). The park-and-ride indicator does not meaningfully improve the model, so the simpler model from Question 12 is preferred.
